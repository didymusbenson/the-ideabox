# Crimson Heart — Worldbuilding

> Story bible for the loom book project. Distilled from the idea docs one level up

> Those remain the source of truth; keep this in sync as drafting reveals new detail.

## Premise (one breath)

A magical-girl team saved the world and grew up. Years later the threats return —
and the retired, endgame-powered, grief-scarred **Crimson Heart** (Bunny Shinzo)
has to suit back up: not to relive her glory days, but to keep the fight from
swallowing her daughter and the quiet life she fought to build.

## Tone & themes

- **Domestic-first, action-light.** Bunny is fully powered; monster fights end in
  seconds. The weight lands on family, grief, and aftermath — "what does winning keep
  costing her."
- **Being a magical *woman*** (not a girl): motherhood, marriage, loss, PTSD.
- Getting her **husband to believe** the bedtime stories are true.
- **Protecting her daughter** from being recruited.
- Reconnecting with **old friends** who have their own lives.
- Throughline: **processing trauma and healing.**

## World Rules

- **Possession / "falling stars."** Threats arrive as shooting stars that strike
  civilians and hijack them (the ch.1 "star-struck man"). Crim's attack shatters the
  star and frees the host. This is the **Star suit / North Star's** calling card;
  whether all Generals work this way is TBD.
- **Transformation.** Bunny transforms with a hidden **transformation stick** (kept in
  a secret compartment of her purse). Call: **"SHINE HEART POWER! SPARKLE!"**
- **Power level.** Crim is endgame-tier from page one; her ultimate ("EXPLOSIVE HEART
  DEVASTATION") ends fights instantly. Tension is emotional, not martial. See **Magic
  & the Chosen** below for what "endgame" means and what it costs her.

## Magic & the Chosen

**The suits are aesthetic, not a system.** There's no cosmic law *making* the magic
card-suited — it's the visual/thematic skin we build on, full stop. Don't write toward
a hidden rulebook or an explanation for *why* hearts/spades/tarot; treat it like the
color of a superhero's cape. Deliberately hand-waved.

- **How the magic works (the little we say).** Kitty finds people with the right
  quality of heart and **unlocks magic already hidden inside them** — Bunny was flagged
  as having "a pure heart, full of love," and the cat opened it. That's the whole
  mechanism. We do **not** dramatize Bunny's origins or her original exploits; she's
  spent years burying them, and the narrative honors that by leaving them vague.
- **Recruitment = the threat to the daughter.** The same pitch is now aimed at Bunny's
  elder daughter — Kitty can allude to it flatly ("your daughter's got a pure heart —
  her magic would be *so* strong"). It doesn't need to make mechanical sense; it needs
  to be the thing Bunny will burn everything down to prevent. **Keep the stakes
  emotional, not procedural** — don't invent consent rules, inheritance genetics, or a
  way for Bunny to "shield" her by powering up. The horror is simply that the offer
  exists and the kid might say yes.

### "Endgame" — maxed out, not invincible

"Endgame" is a **progression** label, not a power ceiling made of plot armor. Picture
Sailor Moon after every upgrade, weapon, and form she'll ever get — Bunny already did
all that growing, off-page, in a war we don't show. So she enters every fight at
**maximum power** and closes it **immediately** with her strongest finisher. There's no
"training up," no monster-of-the-week escalation on the hero side. The fights are short
because she is, mechanically, *done leveling.*

### The real cost: her body, not a magic tax

The toll of transforming and fighting is **not** mystical — it's **age and chronic
illness.** Bunny has **POTS** (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome). Transforming
and channeling magic **spike her heart rate**; the payment comes due as **dizziness,
lightheadedness, and trouble staying on her feet.**

- **The magic carries her through the action** — mid-fight, powered up, she's steady
  and devastating.
- **The crash lands the instant she lets up.** Power down, adrenaline gone, and the
  room tilts. The cost is always deferred to *after* the win — which is the whole
  point: winning keeps costing her, quietly, where no one's clapping.
- Write this as a **real, un-glamorous disability**, not a power gauge. It's the body
  of a grown woman with an illness doing something built for a teenager.

### What the story is actually about

Not the magic. Not the fights. The magic-girl layer is a delivery system for:
**grief and survivor's guilt** (the suffering she endured in the final battle; losing
her boyfriend), **protecting her daughter** from the life, **healing** — learning to
**let her old friends back in** to support her — and **her husband** trying to show up
for her once he knows the truth, out of his depth against problems (trauma, illness,
a literal war) that are **way over his head.** When in doubt, cut toward *those* beats,
not toward lore.

## Locations
- **The Shinzo home** (US suburb) — couch, kitchen, the front door the cat scratches at.
- **Ms. Neighbor's house** — next door; emergency childcare.
- **The grocery store** — egg-price lament; the star-struck man's first scene.
- **Main Street (downtown)** — the crash/chaos; site of Crim's first on-page fight.

## History & Lore
- Crimson Heart's team beat the **Demon Gang** and disbanded into civilian life.
- In the **final battle**, Bunny's love interest (tuxedo-mask analog) died saving her.
  Her trauma is **survivor's guilt + PTSD** from the suffering of that fight and his loss.
- Bunny emigrated to the **US**, married, had two daughters, and buried the past —
  until the shooting stars and the talking cat came back.
