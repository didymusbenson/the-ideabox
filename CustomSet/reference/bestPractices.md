# Best Practices for Magic: The Gathering Set Design

A comprehensive reference document synthesizing Mark Rosewater's expert opinions, design wisdom, and accumulated best practices from over two decades of leading Magic design. Every major point is sourced from specific articles, columns, or talks.

---

## I. Rosewater's Twenty Lessons

Presented at GDC 2016 and published as a three-part Making Magic series, these twenty lessons represent the distilled wisdom of twenty years designing a single game. They are not Magic-specific — they are universal game design principles forged in the crucible of the world's most complex commercial card game.

*Sources: "Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons — Part 1" (2016-05-30), "Part 2" (2016-06-06), "Part 3" (2016-06-13), GDC 2016 Talk*

**Note on numbering:** The GDC talk and the three-part article series present these lessons in slightly different orders. The numbering below follows the article trilogy as the definitive written source. The GDC talk version includes some lessons in different positions and occasionally presents overlapping concepts as distinct lessons (for example, "Don't talk at your audience, talk with them" appears as a separate lesson in some GDC summaries but is woven into Lessons 10 and 13 in the articles).

### Lesson 1: Fighting Against Human Nature Is a Losing Battle

Players have pre-existing behaviors, expectations, and instincts. When a mechanic or rule fights those instincts, the mechanic loses — not the player. The suspend mechanic in Time Spiral illustrated this: players kept wanting to attack with their suspended creatures, even though the rules didn't allow it. The design was fighting what players naturally wanted to do.

**The rule:** "Don't change your players to match your game. Change your game to match your players."

If players consistently misplay a card or misunderstand a mechanic, the fault lies with the design, not with the players. Human psychology is a constant; your game is the variable.

### Lesson 2: Aesthetics Matter

Aesthetics are not cosmetic — they are structural. Humans have deep, often unconscious responses to symmetry, balance, pattern, and visual harmony. The card Griselbrand drew complaints because its mana cost (4BBBB) and stats (7/7) felt asymmetrical and wrong, even though the card was mechanically powerful.

Players expect the components of a game to "feel right." A card with awkward numbers, an ugly mana cost, or a dissonant name-to-mechanic relationship will be received poorly regardless of its gameplay quality. The total sensory package — name, art, rules text, mana cost, power/toughness — determines the player experience.

### Lesson 3: Resonance Is Important

Players arrive with pre-loaded emotional connections. When a card taps into something the player already knows — a fantasy trope, a cultural reference, a genre convention — the card becomes immediately graspable and emotionally satisfying. Innistrad's success was built on resonance: players already knew what vampires, werewolves, and zombies were supposed to feel like, so the set could build on that foundation rather than having to teach everything from scratch.

Resonance reduces cognitive load. A card called "Raise Dead" that returns a creature from the graveyard to your hand communicates itself through its name alone. This is an enormous design advantage.

### Lesson 4: Make Use of Piggybacking

Piggybacking is "the use of pre-existing knowledge to front-load game information to make learning easier." It is the mechanical application of resonance. The keyword "flying" works because everyone understands intuitively that things in the air are hard to reach from the ground. Players don't need to read rules text to "get" it.

The Trojan Horse example from Theros is instructive: during playtesting, the same card was conceptualized as different objects. When presented as "Akroan Horse" — a reference to the Trojan Horse — players instantly understood that it was a gift to your opponent that would eventually betray them. When it had a different name, players were confused by the same mechanics. The flavor was doing mechanical communication work.

### Lesson 5: Don't Confuse "Interesting" with "Fun"

A mechanic can be intellectually stimulating, strategically deep, and conceptually brilliant — and still feel terrible to play. The Odyssey block's mechanics challenged fundamental card advantage theory by rewarding players for discarding cards and having fewer cards in hand. This was "interesting" — it subverted expectations. But it wasn't fun, because it made players feel bad about the things they naturally wanted to do (draw cards, keep a full hand).

"Humans are much more motivated in their decision-making by emotions rather than thoughts." A player's intellectual appreciation of a clever mechanic is no substitute for the visceral joy of playing it.

### Lesson 6: Understand What Emotion Your Game Is Trying to Evoke

Every component of a set should contribute to its intended emotional experience. If an element doesn't serve the emotional goal, it must go — no matter how clever it is individually.

"No scene is worth a movie, no line is worth a scene." This screenwriting adage applies directly to set design: no card is worth a set. A brilliant card that disrupts the set's emotional coherence is doing more harm than good.

Innistrad's emotional center was dread and suspense — the feeling of being hunted. Every mechanic (morbid, flashback, transform) reinforced that core emotion. Cards that didn't contribute to the horror atmosphere were cut, even if they were individually strong designs.

### Lesson 7: Allow the Players the Ability to Make the Game Personal

Players feel more positively about a game when they can shape it to reflect themselves. Magic's modular nature — different formats, deck archetypes, card choices, play styles — allows every player to build a version of the game that feels like theirs.

The more choices you provide — different resources, different paths, the ability to choose and not choose things — the more players will feel the game is "about them." This is why deckbuilding is so central to Magic's appeal: the deck is the player's self-expression.

### Lesson 8: The Details Are Where the Players Fall in Love with Your Game

Players may appreciate the big picture, but they bond with the details. Fblthp, a minor character in the art of a forgettable card, became a beloved meme and eventually got his own card — because players latched onto a tiny, specific detail.

The difference between a 2/3 and a 3/2 on a creature can change everything about how it plays. Mana cost, creature type, exact wording of abilities — these "small" details are where players actually live. Sweat them.

### Lesson 9: Allow Your Players to Have a Sense of Ownership

Players become deeply attached to things they feel they own, created, or discovered. The Commander format thrives because building a 100-card singleton deck around a chosen legendary creature is an intensely personal act. The deck is yours in a way that copying a netdeck never is.

Design should provide tools for personal expression, not just content to consume. Customization, personalization, and the ability to build something uniquely your own are powerful engagement drivers.

### Lesson 10: Leave Room for the Player to Explore

Don't show players everything. Don't telegraph every interaction. Let them discover things themselves.

The two-card combo of Hive Mind and Summoner's Pact creates an unexpected game-winning scenario that isn't obvious from reading either card individually. When a player discovers this interaction on their own, the excitement of discovery is far more powerful than if the game had pointed it out explicitly.

Players are more invested in things they initiated, found, or figured out. Design should create the conditions for discovery, not just deliver answers.

### Lesson 11: If Everyone Likes Your Game but No One Loves It, It Will Fail

Mild universal approval is worse than passionate polarization. Things that evoke strong positive responses almost always also evoke strong negative responses from other players — and that's acceptable. The goal is not to avoid all negative reactions; it's to ensure some players are deeply, passionately in love.

A card with ratings of 50% love it / 50% hate it is more valuable than a card with 100% "it's fine." The passionate fans are the ones who champion the game, build communities, and keep playing.

### Lesson 12: Don't Design to Prove You Can Do Something

Having an ego is fine. Letting your ego drive design decisions is not. Tibalt, the first two-mana planeswalker, was designed primarily to prove it was possible to make one — not because the game needed it. The result was a card that was poorly received because the design goal was proving a point rather than serving the player experience.

"The goal of good game design is to deliver an optimal experience for your target audience." Every decision must serve the game and its players, not the designer's desire for a personal achievement.

### Lesson 13: Make the Fun Part Also the Correct Strategy to Win

Players will always optimize toward winning. If the most effective strategy is also the most boring one, players will be bored. If the fun path and the winning path diverge, players will choose winning and resent the game for making them abandon fun.

The "gotcha" mechanic in Unhinged penalized players for saying specific words. The optimal strategy was to stop talking — which made the game less social and less fun. The mechanic's correct play pattern actively worked against its intended fun.

**The rule:** The fun thing and the optimal thing must be the same thing. If they diverge, fix the design.

### Lesson 14: Don't Be Afraid to Be Blunt

Subtlety is a virtue in many contexts. Game design isn't always one of them. Sometimes you need to be direct to ensure players understand what they're supposed to do.

Ulamog's Crusher has a mandatory attack clause because without it, players would hold back the enormous creature defensively — missing the entire point of the card. The blunt instruction "attacks each combat if able" forced the intended play pattern and taught players how Eldrazi were supposed to feel.

If playtesting reveals that players consistently miss the point of a mechanic, make the point more obvious. Clarity over cleverness.

### Lesson 15: Design the Component for Its Intended Audience

Not every card is for every player. Trying to make a single card appeal to every psychographic simultaneously usually results in a card that appeals to none of them. Molten Sentry tried to be exciting for Timmy (random, dramatic effect) and strategic for Spike (meaningful choices) — and failed at both.

"When you aim to please everyone, you often please no one." Identify which player a card is for, and optimize for that player. Other cards in the set serve other players.

### Lesson 16: Be More Afraid of Boring Your Players Than Challenging Them

Players will forgive ambitious failures. They will not forgive boredom. Split cards were initially resisted within R&D — they were weird, they didn't look like normal Magic cards, they challenged conventions. But players loved them because they were genuinely new and exciting.

"The greatest risk is not taking risks." Playing it safe feels safe, but it leads to the slow death of player interest. Challenge players. Surprise them. Some experiments will fail, but the alternative — never trying anything new — is far worse.

### Lesson 17: You Don't Have to Change Much to Change Everything

Small modifications can have dramatic effects. A single word change in a mechanic's rules text can transform how it plays. Rosewater compares this to overfilling a pot of frozen peas — one more pea and the whole pile shifts.

This lesson is about efficiency in design. When something isn't working, the fix is often much smaller than you think. Don't rebuild from scratch when a tweak will suffice. Conversely, be aware that even tiny changes can cascade through the entire set's dynamics.

### Lesson 18: Restrictions Breed Creativity

Rosewater's most famous principle. Constraints are not obstacles — they are engines of invention. When you have unlimited options, the brain gravitates toward familiar solutions. When you are forced to work within tight boundaries, the brain finds novel paths.

"If you want your brain to get to new places, start from somewhere you've never started before." The color pie, the rarity system, the design skeleton, the mechanical budget — these constraints don't limit good design. They make it possible.

Theme weeks for his Making Magic column force him to explore topics he never would have chosen freely, and some of his best articles have come from those restrictions.

### Lesson 19: Your Audience Is Good at Recognizing Problems and Bad at Solving Them

Listen to player complaints. Take them seriously. But treat their proposed solutions with skepticism. Players know when something feels wrong — that diagnostic ability is invaluable. But they lack the full context of the game's design, the constraints of the system, and the second-order effects of any change.

"A doctor asks how you are feeling because you know yourself best. But they don't ask you how to solve it because they know that better." Use your audience to identify problems; use your expertise to solve them.

### Lesson 20: All the Lessons Connect

These twenty lessons are not independent principles — they form an interconnected web. Resonance (Lesson 3) enables piggybacking (Lesson 4). Understanding emotion (Lesson 6) prevents confusing interesting with fun (Lesson 5). Restrictions (Lesson 18) force details (Lesson 8) that drive player love.

"Everything I've learned all clicks together." Good design is holistic. Each principle reinforces and depends on the others.

## II. The Color Pie as Design Constitution

The color pie is the most important structural element in Magic: The Gathering. Rosewater has called it the game's "constitution" — the foundational law that keeps mechanical identity coherent across tens of thousands of cards. It is not flavor. It is not lore. It is the architectural backbone of the entire game.

*Sources: "Mechanical Color Pie 2021," "Let's Talk Color Pie," "The Big Picture" (2022), "Design 104"*

### Why the Color Pie Matters

The color pie serves three interconnected functions:

1. **Mechanical Identity.** Each color has things it does well, things it does poorly, and things it cannot do at all. This means every color feels distinct to play, and choosing a color is a meaningful strategic decision with real tradeoffs.

2. **Strategic Depth.** Because no color can do everything, players must make choices about what tools they want access to. Two-color decks gain flexibility but lose consistency. Five-color decks theoretically do everything but struggle with mana. These tradeoffs are the game's strategic core.

3. **Design Sustainability.** With 30+ years of cards, the color pie prevents the game from collapsing into sameness. If every color could do everything, colors would lose meaning and the game would lose its structural foundation. The color pie ensures that each new set can create meaningful variety.

**What happens when the color pie breaks:** When a color gets an ability it shouldn't have, non-enfranchised players become confused about what colors represent. Enfranchised players lose trust in the design team's discipline. And the strategic ecosystem degrades — if blue gets efficient creature removal, why play black? Pie breaks undermine every level of the game simultaneously.

### Terminology

- **Primary:** The color(s) that use this ability most frequently, at the highest volume and usually the lowest rarity. The primary color will almost always get this effect in any given set.
- **Secondary:** The color(s) that use this ability on a regular basis, but not as often and not always at as low a rarity as the primary.
- **Tertiary:** The color(s) that use this ability only occasionally, often with strict limitations, higher rarity, or special set-specific reasons.

### White — Order, Community, Protection

**Philosophy:** White believes in the good of the group over the individual. It seeks peace through structure, law, and shared sacrifice. White will protect the community even at great personal cost — but it can also become authoritarian and intolerant in its pursuit of order.

**Primary Mechanical Identity:**
- Small creature swarms and token generation
- Life gain
- Enchantment and artifact removal
- "Rules-setting" effects (taxing, restrictions, hate bears)
- +1/+1 counters and anthem effects (boosting all your creatures)
- Exile-based removal (especially conditional: "destroy target tapped creature," Banisher Priest effects)
- Damage prevention and protection
- Board wipes (destroy all creatures)

**Evergreen Keywords:** Flying, vigilance, first strike, lifelink, double strike

**What White Cannot Do:**
- Efficient unconditional card draw (white's greatest weakness)
- Direct damage to players
- Unconditional land destruction
- Unrestricted graveyard recursion

### Blue — Knowledge, Intellect, Perfection

**Philosophy:** Blue believes that anything can be improved through knowledge, study, and technology. It values information, planning, and control. Blue's weakness is arrogance — the belief that intelligence alone can solve every problem.

**Primary Mechanical Identity:**
- Card draw and card selection (scry, surveil, loot — draw then discard)
- Counterspells
- Bounce effects (returning permanents to hand)
- Flying creatures (the most blue keyword)
- Copying spells and permanents
- Stealing/gaining control of permanents
- Mill (putting cards from library to graveyard)
- Extra turns
- Transformation effects (turning creatures into other things)

**Evergreen Keywords:** Flying, flash, ward, hexproof (tertiary)

**What Blue Cannot Do:**
- Efficient creature removal (blue can bounce or transform, but not destroy)
- Direct damage
- Permanent-based land destruction
- Consistent graveyard recursion

### Black — Ambition, Self-Interest, Power at Any Price

**Philosophy:** Black believes in achieving its goals by any means necessary. It is willing to pay any price — life, creatures, cards — for power. Black is the most pragmatic color, but its ruthlessness can become self-destructive.

**Primary Mechanical Identity:**
- Creature destruction (the primary removal color)
- Drain effects (life loss + life gain)
- Discard (forcing opponents to lose cards from hand)
- Graveyard recursion and reanimation
- Sacrifice effects (both self-sacrifice and forcing opponents to sacrifice)
- Tutoring (searching library for specific cards)
- Drawing cards at the cost of life
- -X/-X effects and debuffs

**Evergreen Keywords:** Flying, deathtouch, menace, lifelink

**What Black Cannot Do:**
- Destroy artifacts (black can only destroy "living" permanents — creatures, planeswalkers, lands)
- Destroy enchantments (this is black's most important weakness)
- Efficient unconditional counterspells
- Broad creature pumping/anthem effects

**Critical Design Note:** Black has the broadest philosophical pie because it's the color willing to do anything — but its mechanical pie is restricted by the rule that it cannot interact with "nonliving" permanents (artifacts, enchantments). This distinction between philosophical breadth and mechanical restriction is essential to black's identity.

### Red — Emotion, Impulse, Freedom

**Philosophy:** Red acts on feeling, not reason. It values freedom, passion, and living in the moment. Red's weakness is shortsightedness — it gets what it wants now at the expense of the future.

**Primary Mechanical Identity:**
- Direct damage (burn spells — red's defining ability)
- Artifact destruction
- Haste (the most red keyword)
- Temporary theft ("Act of Treason" effects — gain control until end of turn)
- Impulsive draw (exile top cards, play them until end of turn — red's unique card advantage solution)
- Land destruction
- Randomness and chaos effects
- Extra combat phases
- Wheel effects (everyone discards hand, draws new cards)

**Evergreen Keywords:** Haste, first strike, menace, trample

**What Red Cannot Do:**
- Destroy enchantments (shared weakness with black)
- Gain life in significant amounts
- Draw cards in a sustained, reliable way (impulsive draw is deliberately temporary)
- Counter spells
- Create lasting board advantages (red's effects are often temporary)

### Green — Nature, Growth, Acceptance

**Philosophy:** Green believes the natural order is already correct. Growth comes from within, from accepting your inherent nature rather than trying to change it. Green values community, interconnection, and the circle of life. Its weakness is resistance to change and complacency.

**Primary Mechanical Identity:**
- Mana acceleration and ramp (the primary ramp color)
- Large creatures (green gets the best stats-for-cost)
- Enchantment and artifact destruction ("Naturalize" effects)
- Fight mechanics (creature-based removal)
- +1/+1 counters and creature growth
- Creature tutoring (searching library for creatures)
- Land tutoring
- Trample and overrun effects

**Evergreen Keywords:** Trample, reach, deathtouch, vigilance (secondary)

**What Green Cannot Do:**
- Direct damage to creatures or players (green must use creatures to fight)
- Counterspells
- Flying (green is explicitly the anti-flying color, which is why it gets reach)
- Steal or copy permanents
- Efficient non-creature removal

### The Council of Colors

Rosewater created the Council of Colors — a rotating group within R&D where each color has a representative who advocates for that color's mechanical identity. Members serve roughly two years before rotating, ensuring fresh perspectives. This institutional structure exists because the color pie is too important to leave unguarded. Without active stewardship, colors would gradually bleed into each other as individual card designers make "small exceptions" that accumulate into identity erosion.

## III. Player Psychographics

Understanding who plays Magic — and why — is essential to designing for them. Rosewater developed and refined a system of player psychographics (motivational profiles) and aesthetic profiles (appreciation styles) that R&D uses to ensure every set serves the full breadth of the playerbase.

*Sources: "Timmy, Johnny, and Spike" (2002, revised 2013), "Vorthos and Mel" (2015-08-31), "The Big Picture" (2022), "Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons — Part 2"*

### The Three Psychographics

Psychographics answer the question "**why** does this player play?" They describe motivation, not behavior. A psychographic is about the psychological need the game fulfills.

#### Timmy/Tammy — The Experience Gamer

**Core Motivation:** Timmy/Tammy plays to *experience* something. The game is an opportunity to feel emotions that everyday life doesn't provide — awe, power, excitement, drama.

**What They Want:**
- Big creatures, big spells, big moments
- Cards that create dramatic, memorable game states
- The feeling of overwhelming power
- Emotional highs — the thrill of casting a massive spell or attacking with an enormous creature

**Cards That Serve Timmy/Tammy:** Enormous dragons, game-ending spells, effects that make you feel powerful. Timmy/Tammy doesn't want to eke out a narrow advantage — they want to *smash*.

**Design Lesson:** Timmy/Tammy cards need impact. A 6/6 flyer for 6 mana that "just" attacks and blocks may be strategically sound but emotionally flat. Give the big creature a dramatic ability. Make the moment feel special.

**Subtypes:**
- **Power Gamer:** Wants the biggest, most impressive cards
- **Social Gamer:** Plays for the social experience; the game is an excuse to hang out
- **Diversity Gamer:** Wants to try everything; plays many formats, many decks
- **Adrenaline Gamer:** Lives for dramatic, uncertain moments; loves randomness and high stakes

#### Johnny/Jenny — The Creative Gamer

**Core Motivation:** Johnny/Jenny plays to *express* something. The game is a canvas for self-expression. Building a deck is an act of creativity, and winning with a unique strategy is proof of that creativity.

**What They Want:**
- Cards with unusual effects that suggest unexpected applications
- Open-ended designs that reward creative deckbuilding
- The ability to win in ways no one expects
- Cards that other players undervalue or overlook

**Cards That Serve Johnny/Jenny:** Cards with unique effects, build-around enchantments, engines, combo pieces. Johnny/Jenny doesn't want to play the "best" deck — they want to play *their* deck.

"Johnny likes a challenge. Johnny enjoys winning with cards that no one else wants to use."

**Design Lesson:** Johnny/Jenny cards need open-endedness. A card that does exactly one thing in exactly one way doesn't serve the creative gamer. Give them tools, not solutions. Leave room for them to find the interactions themselves (connects to Lesson 10: Leave Room to Explore).

**Subtypes:**
- **Combo Player:** Finds and assembles card interactions
- **Offbeat Designer:** Builds decks around unusual themes or constraints
- **Deck Artist:** Treats the deck as an aesthetic object
- **Uber Johnny/Jenny:** Pushes creative deckbuilding to extreme limits

#### Spike — The Competitive Gamer

**Core Motivation:** Spike plays to *prove* something. The game is a test of skill, and winning demonstrates mastery. Spike seeks the best possible outcome and respects the game's strategic depth.

**What They Want:**
- Cards that are objectively powerful and efficient
- Tools that reward skill-intensive play
- A metagame that allows preparation and adaptation to pay off
- The feeling that their wins are earned through superior decision-making

**Cards That Serve Spike:** Efficiently costed creatures, versatile removal, cards that offer meaningful choices between options. Spike wants cards that are powerful in the hands of a skilled player and weaker in the hands of an unskilled one — cards that reward expertise.

"Spike plays to win. Spike enjoys winning."

**Design Lesson:** Spike cards need depth. A card that's powerful without requiring any decisions is less appealing to Spike than a card that's powerful *when played correctly*. Give Spike cards that reward reading the game state, timing, and metagame knowledge.

**Subtypes:**
- **Innovator:** Finds the best deck before anyone else
- **Tuner:** Takes an existing deck and optimizes it to perfection
- **Analyst:** Studies the metagame and adapts
- **Nut & Bolt Spike:** Focuses on technical in-game play (sequencing, bluffing, math)

### Hybrid Psychographics

Most players are combinations:
- **Timmy/Spike:** Wants to win *big* — not just win, but do it with dramatic flair
- **Johnny/Spike:** Wants to win *creatively* — rogue deckbuilders who bring unexpected strategies to tournaments
- **Timmy/Johnny:** Wants to build something unique *and* experience the emotional thrill of it working
- **Timmy/Johnny/Spike:** The rare player who wants all three — a unique deck that's competitively viable and creates dramatic game moments

### The Two Aesthetic Profiles

Aesthetic profiles answer a different question: "**what** does this player find beautiful in the game?" They are orthogonal to psychographics — you can be a Timmy Vorthos, a Spike Mel, a Johnny Vorthos, etc.

#### Vorthos — Flavor Appreciator

**What They Appreciate:** The creative elements of Magic — the worldbuilding, the art, the names, the flavor text, the story, the character design. Vorthos finds beauty in how cards represent their source material. They care about whether a vampire card *feels* like a vampire.

Vorthos values:
- Card names that evoke the world
- Art that tells a story
- Flavor text that deepens lore
- Mechanics that express flavor (top-down design)
- Creative consistency within a set and across the multiverse

"Beauty is seen through flavor representation."

#### Mel — Mechanical Appreciator

**What They Appreciate:** The structural craft of Magic design — the elegance of the color pie, the interlocking mechanical systems, the templating, the mana curve, the way all the pieces fit together. Mel finds beauty in *how a card was designed*, not what it represents.

Mel values:
- Elegant mechanical interactions
- Color pie fidelity
- Clean templating
- Structural coherence across the set
- Cards where every element (cost, power/toughness, abilities, rarity) feels precisely calibrated

"Beauty is seen in how it was structurally put together."

### How to Serve All Players in One Set

The key insight from "The Big Picture" (2022): **"Design Magic so that each player has the tools to make it the game they love."**

This does not mean every card must appeal to every player. It means:

1. **Every set must contain cards for each psychographic.** Timmy/Tammy gets their splashy rares. Johnny/Jenny gets their build-around uncommons. Spike gets efficiently costed tools. Vorthos gets resonant top-down designs. Mel gets elegant mechanical interactions.

2. **Individual cards should be designed for their intended audience** (Lesson 15). A card trying to please everyone usually pleases no one. Know who the card is for and optimize for that player.

3. **"If everyone likes your game but no one loves it, it will fail"** (Lesson 11). Some cards will polarize. That's acceptable — even desirable — as long as the passionate fans outnumber the indifferent ones.

4. **Magic is not one game but a game system.** Different formats (Standard, Commander, Draft, Modern) serve different psychographic profiles. The system's modularity is itself a design choice that enables universal service.

## IV. New World Order

New World Order (NWO) is R&D's framework for managing complexity by rarity. Introduced around 2008 and first fully articulated in Rosewater's December 2011 article, it is arguably the single most impactful design policy change in Magic's history.

*Sources: "New World Order" (December 2011 Making Magic), "Nuts & Bolts #4: Higher Rarities," "The Big Picture" (2022), MTG Wiki NWO entry*

### The Problem

By the time of Time Spiral block (2006-2007), Magic's player acquisition rate was declining. R&D investigated and identified three types of complexity that were making the game increasingly hostile to new players:

1. **Comprehension Complexity** — How hard is it to understand what a card does? Can a player read the card and know how it works?

2. **Board Complexity** — How much mental load does a card add to the game state? How many things does a player have to track simultaneously? Cards with multiple triggered abilities, static effects that interact with other cards, and "aura-like" effects that modify other permanents all add board complexity.

3. **Strategic Complexity** — How hard is it to figure out the optimal way to use a card? A card that's easy to understand but difficult to deploy optimally has high strategic complexity.

The key insight: these three types of complexity are independent of each other. A card can be low in comprehension complexity (easy to read) but high in board complexity (hard to track in a game with many permanents). NWO addresses each type differently.

### The Solution: Complexity as a Rarity Resource

The breakthrough was recognizing that rarity — a tool trading card games have always had — could be used to stratify complexity. New players buy fewer boosters. The percentage of their cards that are common is much higher than for enfranchised players. Therefore, if commons are kept simple, new players get a simpler game experience, while experienced players still have access to complex cards at higher rarities.

**The NWO Rules:**

- **Commons must be low in all three types of complexity.** A common should be understandable on first read, easy to track on the board, and have a reasonably intuitive optimal play pattern.

- **Uncommons can have moderate complexity.** This is where set themes get expressed with more sophistication, signpost cards guide draft archetypes, and build-around effects live.

- **Rares and mythic rares can be as complex as necessary.** This is where the most intricate, powerful, and strategically deep designs belong.

### The Red Flag System

NWO uses a "red flag" system for commons that exceed normal complexity:

- Any common that has unusual complexity gets "red flagged" during design review
- **Less than 20% of commons should be red-flagged**
- Red-flagged commons that survive must make a critical contribution to the set's theme or limited environment
- If a common is red-flagged and doesn't serve a vital role, it should be simplified or moved to uncommon

### What NWO Is Not

Rosewater has repeatedly clarified what NWO does and does not cover:

- NWO is **only** about complexity at common. It does not restrict higher rarities.
- NWO did not "dumb down" Magic. It shifted where complexity lives — and actually increased complexity at uncommon and above to compensate.
- NWO does not prevent any specific mechanic from existing. It only asks: "Can this mechanic be executed simply enough for common?" If yes, it goes on common cards. If no, it moves to higher rarities.

### How NWO Improved Design for Everyone

The counterintuitive outcome: NWO didn't just help new players. It improved the game for experienced players too:

- **Simpler commons make board states easier to parse.** Even experienced players benefit from not having to track fifteen different triggered abilities on common creatures during a draft game.
- **Complexity at higher rarities feels more special.** When commons are simple, the complex rare you open feels like a genuine step up in sophistication.
- **Draft environments became more readable.** With simpler commons defining the base layer of gameplay, the strategic decisions at higher rarities stand out more clearly.
- **Design became more disciplined.** Treating complexity as a limited resource at common forced designers to be more intentional about every point of complexity they spent.

### Applying NWO to Custom Set Design

When designing commons, ask these questions for every card:

1. **Comprehension:** Can a player who has never seen this card understand it on first read?
2. **Board:** Once this card is in play, how much does it add to what everyone has to track?
3. **Strategy:** Is the right way to use this card reasonably obvious?

If a common scores high on any axis, it's a red flag. Either simplify the card, move it to uncommon, or justify its complexity by proving it's essential to the set's core experience.

## V. The Design Pipeline

Modern Magic design uses a three-stage pipeline. Understanding what each stage does — and crucially, what it does *not* do — is essential for any designer trying to build a set.

*Sources: "Vision Design, Set Design, and Play Design" (2017-10-23), "Stages of Design," "Nuts & Bolts #7: Three Stages of Design," "The Big Picture" (2022)*

### Historical Context

The pipeline has evolved through seven distinct eras:

**First Stage (Alpha through Alliances):** Richard Garfield's era. Individual cards were designed to be exciting in isolation. There was no block structure, no formal color pie management, and the game was designed for small playgroups with a few hundred cards. This era produced brilliant individual cards but inconsistent sets.

**Second Stage (Mirage through Prophecy):** Led by Joel Mick. The rules were standardized (introducing the stack), the color pie was formalized, block structure with large and small sets was established, and Limited formats were designed for intentionally.

**Third Stage (Invasion through Saviors of Kamigawa):** Led by Bill Rose. Blocks were built around specific themes, the Multiverse was explored by creating new planes, and Pro Tour players were hired to improve card balance. Mechanics were named and labeled more aggressively, and successful mechanics started being brought back.

**Fourth Stage (Ravnica through Rise of the Eldrazi):** Led by Rosewater. Block planning improved significantly, with more intentional design across sets within a block. Experimentation with block structures began.

**Fifth Stage (Scars of Mirrodin through Rivals of Ixalan):** The concept of "emotional center" was introduced — each set was designed to evoke a specific feeling. Mechanics and creative became integrated storytelling tools.

**Sixth Stage (Dominaria through Innistrad: Crimson Vow):** The block structure was eliminated entirely. The three-part design system (Vision, Set, Play) was formalized. Increased focus on eternal formats and backward compatibility.

**Seventh Stage (Kamigawa: Neon Dynasty onward):** The current era. "The core of the current stage of design is that Magic design is questioning itself." More willingness to experiment, expanded genre boundaries, and active re-evaluation of long-held assumptions.

### The Modern Three-Stage Pipeline

#### Vision Design (~4-7 months, including exploratory)

**Led by:** Mark Rosewater (for most premier sets)

**What Vision Design Does:**
- Establishes the set's mechanical and thematic identity
- Answers the Three Questions: What is this set about? Why should players care? What makes it different?
- Creates the core mechanics
- Explores broadly, then narrows to a focused vision
- Builds a full set of commons and uncommons plus enough rares/mythics for draft/sealed testing
- Defines the emotional center of the set

**What Vision Design Delivers (The Handoff):**
- A vision document articulating the set's identity, themes, and mechanical direction
- A playable card file with all commons and uncommons, plus representative rares and mythics
- Clear documentation of what the mechanics are, why they exist, and what they're trying to accomplish
- Guidance on what's core to the vision (non-negotiable) vs. what's flexible

**Key Principle:** Vision design is about exploration and identity. It answers "what are we making?" and "why?"

#### Set Design (~9 months)

**Led by:** A dedicated set design lead (historically Erik Lauer and others)

**What Set Design Does:**
- Takes Vision's blueprint and builds the complete card file
- Field-tests every mechanic from Vision; fixes or replaces mechanics that don't work in practice
- Fills out the entire design skeleton across all rarities
- Handles the granular work of card-by-card design
- Manages the as-fan calculations and mechanic density
- Ensures draft archetypes are functional and diverse
- Iterates extensively through playtesting

**What Set Design Delivers:**
- A refined, complete card file ready for Play Design's final tuning
- All mechanics finalized and tested
- Draft archetypes validated
- Creative elements (names, flavor text, art descriptions) integrated

**Key Principle:** Set design is about execution. It answers "how do we build this?" and "does it actually work?"

#### Play Design (~4 months, overlapping with late Set Design)

**Led by:** A play design team focused on competitive balance

**What Play Design Does:**
- Ensures the health of core formats (Standard, Draft, Sealed, and increasingly Commander)
- Evaluates the tournament environment the set will create
- Catches and addresses problem cards before release
- Handles final power-level tuning
- Tests constructed decks against existing format staples
- Manages the delicate balance between cards being exciting enough to matter and not so powerful that they warp formats

**Key Principle:** Play Design is about health. It answers "is this set fun and fair across all the formats it touches?"

### The Full Timeline

The entire process from exploratory design to Play Design completion takes approximately 20 months per set. At any given time, multiple sets are in different stages of the pipeline simultaneously.

### Applying the Pipeline to Custom Design

Even without separate teams, the pipeline's *stages* are invaluable for solo or small-team design:

1. **Don't skip Vision.** Start by defining what the set is about. Don't begin designing individual cards until you can describe the set in seven words or fewer.
2. **Separate generation from evaluation.** Vision is generative; Set Design is evaluative (and then generative within constraints); Play Design is analytical. Don't try to do all three at once.
3. **Respect the handoff.** When moving from vision to execution, document what's core and what's flexible. Future-you needs to know which elements are load-bearing.

## VI. Mechanic Design Best Practices

Designing mechanics — the named or unnamed rules systems that define a set's gameplay identity — is the core craft of Magic design. Rosewater has written extensively about how to find, evaluate, iterate, and finalize mechanics.

*Sources: "Finding a Good Mechanic" (2002), "Keyword Play" (2007), "Keyword to the Wise" (2003), "Nuts & Bolts #12, #17 Parts 1 & 2, #18," "Design 101-104"*

### Finding Mechanics: Seven Paths

There is no single way to discover a mechanic. Rosewater identifies seven distinct paths:

1. **Spontaneous Generation.** A moment of inspiration, often during gameplay. Flashback came from the desire to cast a spell a second time from the graveyard.

2. **Fulfilling Longstanding Desires.** Players and designers have wish lists. "Can't be countered" addressed the longstanding desire to guarantee spell resolution.

3. **Expanding Existing Cards.** Taking a unique card's ability and generalizing it into a keyword. Changeling was generalized from Mistform Ultimus (a creature with every creature type).

4. **Naming Existing Patterns.** Sometimes a pattern already exists across many cards but hasn't been formalized. Giving it a name creates synergy opportunities and player vocabulary.

5. **Designing to Set Theme.** Creating a mechanic specifically to serve the set's concept. Morbid was designed for Innistrad to make death mechanically relevant in a horror set.

6. **Filling Skeleton Gaps.** A mechanic born from structural need. Fabricate in Kaladesh emerged because the set needed a flexible mechanic that could serve multiple roles in the skeleton.

7. **Solving Playtest Problems.** A mechanic created to fix issues discovered during testing. Bestow in Theros solved the longstanding Aura vulnerability problem (auras dying when their target dies).

### Evaluating Mechanics: The Four Tests

Every candidate mechanic must pass four tests:

1. **Is it fun to play?** The most fundamental and non-negotiable test. A mechanic that is clever, resonant, and scalable but not enjoyable to play has failed. This can only be determined through playtesting — never through theorycrafting alone.

2. **How many cards can support it?** Scalability determines whether a mechanic can be a set mechanic (needing 15-30+ cards) or only a one-off. Ask:
   - What card types can it go on? (Creatures only? Instants/sorceries? All types?)
   - Which colors can use it? (All five? Only two or three?)
   - At what rarities can it appear? (Simple enough for common?)
   - A great mechanic that only works on two cards isn't a set mechanic

3. **Can it be tweaked and evolved?** Good mechanics have room for variation. Buyback evolved from colorless mana costs to life costs to multiple alternative costs. Kicker evolved from single-mode to multi-mode to multi-kicker. A mechanic with no knobs to turn will feel static.

4. **What gameplay environment does it create?** Consider the ecosystem, not just individual card interactions. Threshold encouraged aggressive graveyard-filling strategies, which meant the set needed cards that enabled sacrifice, discard, and self-mill. Every mechanic shapes the environment around it.

### The As-Fan Framework

**As-fan** measures how frequently a mechanic appears in an average booster pack. It is the most important structural metric for determining whether a mechanic "feels" like a set theme.

- Track mechanic frequency across rarities
- Calculate expected appearances per booster based on collation math
- **If as-fan is too low:** The mechanic won't feel like a set theme. Players won't encounter it often enough to build around it. It will feel like a random inclusion rather than a defining feature.
- **If as-fan is too high:** The mechanic crowds out other mechanics and makes the set feel one-note. Games become repetitive.
- **Rule of thumb:** Uncommons count as roughly half a common for as-fan calculations (because you see them half as often)

"Getting the as-fan is tricky." This is one of the hardest and most important structural challenges in set design.

### Modularity vs. Linearity

Every mechanic falls somewhere on a spectrum:

- **Modular mechanics** work independently and go in any deck. Flying is modular — a flying creature is good in any deck, regardless of what else is in it. Modular mechanics are easier to design for because they don't require structural support.

- **Linear mechanics** require specific other cards to function. Tribal synergies (e.g., "whenever another Elf enters the battlefield") are linear — they demand that the deck contain other Elves. Linear mechanics create focused deck-building constraints.

**Design implications:**
- Linear mechanics need higher density in their supported colors. If you're doing Elf tribal, there must be *enough* Elves in green and black to make the archetype viable in draft.
- Modular mechanics can be spread thinner because they don't need a critical mass.
- The best sets use a mix: a modular backbone with linear themes concentrated in specific color pairs.
- Concentrate linear mechanics in fewer colors to increase density where they appear, rather than spreading them across all five.

### When to Keyword

From "Keyword Play" (2007) and "Keyword to the Wise" (2003):

**Keyword an ability when:**
- It appears frequently enough to warrant shorthand
- The rules text is long or complex enough to benefit from compression
- You want other cards to reference or interact with it (e.g., "creatures with flying get +1/+1")
- Standardized templating would fix existing inconsistencies across cards
- The name itself adds "coolness" and identity (the keyword becomes a hook)

**Do NOT keyword when:**
- The ability is too narrow or appears on too few cards to justify the cognitive investment
- The keyword would add learning overhead without sufficient payoff
- The ability is simple enough that spelling it out is clearer than a keyword plus reminder text
- The keyword would create future design constraints you don't want (a keyword today interacts with cards designed years from now)

**The counterintuitive truth about keywords:** Keywords *reduce* mental load, not increase it. A keyword like "flying" is one cognitive unit (one "chunk") instead of a full sentence of rules text. Reminder text handles the initial learning curve, and then the keyword becomes pure cognitive compression. The shared vocabulary keywords create is a net positive for accessibility.

### The Layering Process

Once mechanics are identified individually, they must be layered into the set one at a time (from "Nuts & Bolts #18"):

1. **Prioritize mechanics before adding them.** Rank from most to least important by: complexity level, set embodiment (how central to the set's identity), synergy potential, and space requirements.

2. **Add one mechanic at a time.** Never integrate everything simultaneously. Each addition constrains what you can do with subsequent mechanics.

3. **Test after each addition.** Verify the new mechanic works with what's already in the set before adding the next.

4. **Look for connections.** The best sets have mechanics that naturally interact with each other, creating emergent gameplay that feels richer than the sum of its parts.

### Mechanic Budget

A set should have:
- **3-6 named/keyword mechanics**
- **1-4 unnamed mechanics** (recurring themes without keyword labels)
- **A "_______ matters" theme** (e.g., "artifacts matter," "graveyard matters")
- All mechanics must reinforce the set's core concept

More than 6 named mechanics creates cognitive overload. Fewer than 3 makes the set feel mechanically thin.

## VII. The Art of Cutting

The willingness to cut — beloved cards, promising mechanics, entire design directions — is what separates professional design from amateur enthusiasm. Rosewater has called this one of the hardest and most important skills a designer must develop.

*Sources: "Design 104," "Nuts & Bolts #3," "Nuts & Bolts #6," "Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons — Part 1" (Lesson 6), "The Big Picture" (2022)*

### The Cardinal Principle

**"The set is more important than any individual card."** This is non-negotiable. A brilliant card that doesn't fit the set is not a good card *for that set*. A beautiful mechanic that doesn't serve the set's identity must go. No card, no mechanic, no design direction is too good to cut.

"No scene is worth a movie, no line is worth a scene." Every element must serve the whole.

### When to Cut

Cut a card or mechanic when:

1. **It doesn't serve the set's identity.** If the set is about enchantments and a card has nothing to do with enchantments and doesn't serve a structural role (like basic removal), ask why it's there.

2. **It creates anti-synergy with other elements.** The "bombo list" from playtesting (Nuts & Bolts #6) tracks pairs of cards with negative interactions. If a card has multiple bombo interactions, it's working against the set.

3. **It's individually good but collectively harmful.** A powerful, exciting card that warps the draft environment or overshadows the set's themes is doing more harm than good.

4. **Playtesting reveals it isn't fun.** The most common reason for cutting. If a mechanic tests poorly — even if the concept was brilliant on paper — it must go. The Orzhov mechanic in Gatecrash was "a bust" — confusing, didn't hang together — and was completely replaced with Extort.

5. **The mechanic doesn't scale.** If it only works on a handful of cards, it's not a set mechanic. Either generalize it or save it for another context.

6. **Complexity budget is exceeded.** NWO means complexity is a limited resource, especially at common. If the set has too many mechanics, even good ones, something must give.

### The Emotional Discipline

Cutting is emotionally painful. Rosewater is explicit about this: "Good design requires emotionally bonding to the thing you're creating" — you *should* care about your designs. The problem is that the same emotional investment that fuels creative work also makes cutting feel like failure.

It isn't failure. It's craft.

**Strategies for building cutting discipline:**

- **Use emotional distance.** Take time away from the design before evaluating. Fresh eyes see what fatigued ones miss.
- **Involve outside perspectives.** Designers become too close to their own work. Outside playtesters and collaborators provide the objectivity you've lost.
- **Rate cards systematically.** After each playtest, rate every card: Yes / No / Unsure / Didn't Play. Data-driven decisions are easier to make than gut-feeling ones.
- **Track everything.** Keep records of what you've cut and why. This prevents re-arguing settled questions and builds institutional memory.

### When to Save Ideas for Later

Not every cut is a kill. Magic's long-running nature means that ideas that don't work in one set can find a home in another:

- **Chroma** (Eventide) was a mechanic that cared about colored mana symbols on permanents. It was considered a failure in its original form. Years later, it was reworked and renamed **Devotion** for Theros, where it became one of the most beloved mechanics in the set. Same core concept, better execution, better context.

- **Transform (double-faced cards)** was considered for earlier sets but the technology and creative context weren't right. It found its perfect home in Innistrad, where werewolf transformation demanded it.

- The Gatecrash case study is instructive: of five guild mechanics, one was kept as-is (Evolve), one was kept as-is (Battalion), one was iterated on (Grind/Dimir), one went through multiple versions before landing (Bloodrush for Gruul), and one was scrapped entirely and replaced (Extort for Orzhov). The scrapped Orzhov mechanic's concepts may have informed later designs.

**The rule:** Cut ideas from the set, not from existence. Maintain a file of cut designs. Good ideas are patient — they'll wait for the right context.

### The Volume Reality

"The vast majority of cards I create are junk." — Design 102

"Expect to design 40-50 mechanics in exploration to find the one that ships." — Nuts & Bolts #17

This isn't a sign of poor design. It's the nature of the process. A high cut rate is healthy and expected. If you're keeping everything you design, you're either a once-in-a-generation genius or — far more likely — you're not being critical enough.

## VIII. Balancing Innovation and Familiarity

Every Magic set must be new enough to justify its existence and familiar enough to feel like Magic. Finding the right balance on this spectrum is one of the most difficult ongoing challenges in the game's design.

*Sources: "The Big Picture" (2022), "Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons" (Lessons 3, 4, 16, 17, 18), "Stages of Design," "Design 104," Fundamentals synthesis*

### The Innovation Imperative

Magic must constantly innovate to survive. Rosewater is unequivocal about this: "I honestly believe that if [we] stopped innovating, if [we] stopped pushing boundaries and exploring what Magic can be, it would die." A game that plays the same way set after set becomes boring, and bored players leave.

From "The Big Picture" (2022), innovation is one of the six fundamental pillars of modern design philosophy: **"Constantly ask 'What could Magic be?' instead of 'What is Magic?'"** The game should be defined by its potential, not its history.

**Lesson 16: Be more afraid of boring your players than challenging them.** Split cards, double-faced cards, planeswalkers — all of these were internally controversial innovations that became beloved. The fear of trying something new is almost always more dangerous than the thing itself.

### The Familiarity Anchor

But innovation without grounding is disorientation. Players need **anchor points** — familiar structures that give them a foothold from which to explore new territory.

"Somewhat different is good. Too different is alienating." — Design 104

Anchor points come in several forms:

1. **Evergreen mechanics.** Flying, trample, deathtouch, haste — these appear in every set. They provide a baseline vocabulary that every player shares, regardless of when they started playing. New players learn them once; returning players recognize them immediately.

2. **Structural conventions.** The color pie, the mana system, the rarity framework, the creature/spell division — these don't change from set to set. They are the constants that make the variables feel navigable.

3. **Returning mechanics.** Bringing back beloved mechanics (cycling, kicker, flashback) in new sets provides instant familiarity. The mechanic is known, so players can focus their learning energy on the truly new elements.

4. **Resonant themes.** A set themed around vampires, pirates, or Greek mythology provides anchor points that come from outside the game. Players know what vampires are; they don't need to learn the concept from scratch, only how this set expresses it.

### The Comfort/Novelty Spectrum

Rosewater's design philosophy treats every set as a portfolio of elements ranging from completely familiar to radically new:

**Fully Familiar Elements:**
- Evergreen keywords (flying, vigilance, etc.)
- Basic game structures (lands, creatures, spells)
- Returning mechanics from previous sets
- Archetype patterns players have seen before (aggro, control, midrange)

**Moderately New Elements:**
- New keyword mechanics (but built on comprehensible foundations)
- New draft archetypes (but using familiar building blocks)
- Twists on known concepts (a familiar mechanic in an unexpected color)

**Radically New Elements:**
- New card types or supertypes
- Mechanics that use game resources in unprecedented ways
- Structural innovations (double-faced cards, split cards, adventures)
- "Rule-breaking" designs that challenge what players think Magic can do

**The balance rule:** Every set should have elements in each category. Too much in the "fully familiar" bucket and the set is boring. Too much in the "radically new" bucket and the set is overwhelming. The sweet spot has a familiar base, a healthy amount of moderate novelty, and one or two radical innovations that serve as the set's identity.

### How Much New Is Too Much?

From Rosewater's accumulated wisdom, several guidelines emerge:

1. **One radical innovation per set is usually right.** Innistrad had double-faced cards. Kaladesh had energy. Ikoria had mutate. One big, new, "never done before" thing gives the set its identity without overwhelming players with novelty.

2. **3-6 named mechanics is the budget** (Nuts & Bolts #15). This includes both new and returning mechanics. A set with six brand-new mechanics and zero returning ones is almost certainly too much. A mix — say, two new mechanics and two returning mechanics — provides the right balance.

3. **New mechanics should be piggybacked** (Lesson 4). The more unfamiliar a mechanic is, the more important it is that it connects to something the player already knows. Double-faced cards worked in Innistrad because werewolf transformation was the concept — players understood why these cards existed before they read the rules.

4. **Innovation must serve the set's identity** (Design 104). "New is not the same as improved." Don't add a radical mechanic because it's novel. Add it because the set needs it. If the set concept doesn't demand innovation in a particular area, use a known tool.

5. **Lesson 17: You don't have to change much to change everything.** Often the right amount of innovation is smaller than you think. A single new keyword, applied well, can transform how a set plays. You don't need to reinvent every aspect of the game to make a set feel new.

### The Self-Questioning Era

In "Stages of Design," Rosewater describes the current era (Kamigawa: Neon Dynasty onward) as one where "Magic design is questioning itself." This means:

- Previously sacrosanct assumptions are being re-examined
- Genre boundaries are expanding (cyberpunk in Kamigawa, Western in Thunder Junction)
- Complexity calibration is being reconsidered (some commons are getting slightly more complex again)
- The definition of "what Magic can be" is broader than ever

This doesn't mean anything goes. The color pie remains inviolable. The fundamental gameplay loop is unchanged. But within those constraints, the range of permissible novelty has expanded — because the design team has developed the skill and the institutional confidence to manage it.

## IX. Connecting the Principles

These eight areas of design wisdom are not independent silos. They form an interconnected web — exactly as Lesson 20 predicts.

### How Everything Fits Together

- **The color pie** creates the **restrictions** (Lesson 18) that breed creative mechanic design.
- **New World Order** ensures that innovation happens at the right **rarity**, serving all **psychographic** types by keeping the entry-level experience accessible.
- **The design pipeline** institutionalizes the separation of **generation and evaluation** that prevents premature cutting and ensures thorough exploration.
- **As-fan** and the **design skeleton** are the structural tools that translate abstract vision into concrete card files.
- **Resonance** (Lesson 3) and **piggybacking** (Lesson 4) are the mechanisms that allow **innovation** to land — by anchoring new concepts in familiar understanding.
- **Cutting** is the discipline that prevents a set from collapsing under the weight of too many good ideas — because "the set is more important than any individual card."
- **Player psychographics** ensure that the cutting is targeted: every card that survives serves a specific audience, and every audience is served.

### The Master Heuristic

If there is a single sentence that captures Rosewater's design philosophy, it may be this one from "The Big Picture" (2022):

**"Design Magic so that each player has the tools to make it the game they love."**

That's it. That's the job. The color pie gives the tools structure. Psychographics identify who the players are. New World Order ensures the tools are accessible. The pipeline ensures the tools are well-crafted. Mechanic design creates the tools. Cutting ensures only the right tools ship. And the balance of innovation and familiarity ensures the tools feel both fresh and trustworthy.
