# Design Dos and Don'ts: A Practical Reference for Magic Set Design

A comprehensive, actionable guide for designing Magic: The Gathering sets. Every point is traceable to source material from Mark Rosewater's Making Magic columns, official Wizards of the Coast articles, and established community wisdom. Organized for quick lookup.

---

## DOs

### Ideation Phase

**DO describe your set in seven words or less before designing a single card.**
Your set needs a clear, communicable identity. If you cannot articulate what it is about in a single sentence, the set will drift and become incoherent. Kaladesh's vision was "Feel like an optimistic aetherpunk inventor." Everything in the set should flow from this focal point. Without a clear path, you will meander.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #9: Evaluation; Nuts & Bolts #14: Initial Ideation*

**DO answer the three essential questions before beginning full design.**
(1) What is this set about? (2) Why should players care? (3) What makes this set different from every other set? If you cannot answer all three clearly and compellingly, the set is not ready for design. These are not optional — they are the foundation everything else rests on.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #9: Evaluation; Design 104*

**DO commit to a focal point and build outward from it.**
The idea itself matters less than the commitment to focus on it. Ravnica started with a single premise: "Build a multicolor block around ten equally treated two-color pairs." Everything else — guilds, mechanics, flavor — flowed from that. Having a direction, even an imperfect one, is infinitely better than having none.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #14: Initial Ideation*

**DO list all player expectations for your theme and prioritize them.**
When you choose a well-known theme, players arrive with strong expectations. List every trope and concept the audience expects, have team members vote on the most compelling, and create a prioritized list of must-haves versus nice-to-haves. Meeting obvious expectations is not boring — it is satisfying.
*Source: Top Down and Goal; A View From the Top; Nuts & Bolts #14*

**DO design for emotion, not intellect.**
The goal of every mechanic and card is to make players feel something — excitement, wonder, cleverness, dread. A mechanic can be clever and still feel bad to play. The fundamental test is not "is this interesting?" but "is this fun?" "Interesting" and "fun" are not synonyms.
*Source: Stages of Design; Ten Principles of Good Design, Part 1*

**DO generate lots of ideas before evaluating any of them.**
Generation and evaluation are fundamentally different cognitive modes. Trying to do both simultaneously cripples both. In the early phase, produce quantity without judgment. Expect most of what you create to be junk — that is normal and expected. Design 40-50 mechanics in exploration to find the one that ships.
*Source: Design 102; The Blank Page; Nuts & Bolts #17*

### Skeleton Building Phase

**DO build and use a design skeleton.**
The design skeleton is the most important tool in set design. It maps every card slot by color, rarity, and type, ensuring nothing critical is forgotten. It is a living document — adapt it as the set takes shape — but always have one.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #2: Design Skeleton; Nuts & Bolts #13: Design Skeleton Revisited*

**DO fill commons first and tackle the hardest elements first.**
Commons define the play experience and carry the set's identity. Fill them first. Within commons, prioritize: (1) new mechanics (most constrained), (2) multicolor cards, (3) cycle cards, (4) cards with multiple simultaneous requirements. Lock in the hardest elements before the flexible ones.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #3: Filling In the Design Skeleton*

**DO maintain proper creature counts and mana curves.**
Target approximately 50% creatures across the set. Each color has its own creature ratio (White highest at ~62% at common, Blue lowest at ~50%). Distribute creatures across small/medium/large sizes and across mana values 1-5+. A missing two-drop in white or a missing common flyer in blue creates real gameplay problems.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #2; Nuts & Bolts #13; MTGNexus: So You Want to Build a Set*

**DO include a 10-card two-color signpost uncommon cycle.**
One signpost card for each color pair tells drafters what that archetype is about. Under Play Booster structure, consider two multicolor uncommons per pair — an enabler and a payoff. These are essential signals for the Limited environment.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #13; Nuts & Bolts #16; MTGNexus: Draft Archetypes*

**DO plan your structural infrastructure.**
Every set needs invisible support systems that most players never consciously notice: mana fixing, mana sinks (things to do with excess mana), deck smoothing (draw, scry, cycling), evasion abilities to break stalls, and a balanced ecosystem of threats and answers. These are not exciting, but their absence is devastating.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #15: Structural Support; Nuts & Bolts #16*

### Mechanic Design Phase

**DO make sure your theme is visible at common.**
If your theme only shows up at rare, most players will never experience it. The identity of a set lives or dies at common. Commons are seen most frequently, define the draft experience, and must carry the set's identity. If you cannot see your theme at common, you do not have a theme.
*Source: Design 102; Nuts & Bolts #3*

**DO test every mechanic with four questions.**
(1) Is it fun to play? (2) How many cards can support it? (3) Can it be tweaked and evolved? (4) What gameplay environment does it create? A mechanic that fails any of these is suspect. A great mechanic that only works on two cards is not a set mechanic.
*Source: Keyword Play; Finding a Good Mechanic; Nuts & Bolts #17*

**DO start mechanic testing with commons and uncommons.**
If the mechanic does not work at the most basic level, it will not work at higher rarities. Build 40-card playtesting decks with 4-8 cards using the new mechanic and fill the rest with known reprints to isolate the mechanic's impact.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #17, Part 2*

**DO layer mechanics into the set one at a time.**
Never integrate everything simultaneously. Add the most important or most constrained mechanic first, then the next, and so on. Each mechanic you add constrains what you can do with subsequent ones. Prioritize by: complexity level, how well it embodies the set, synergy potential, and space requirements.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #18: Layering Your Mechanics*

**DO track as-fan for every mechanic.**
As-fan measures how frequently a mechanic appears in an average booster pack. If as-fan is too low, the mechanic will not feel like a set theme. If too high, it crowds out everything else. Getting the as-fan right is one of the hardest and most important structural challenges in set design.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #12; Nuts & Bolts #17*

**DO design "glue" cards that bridge between your themes.**
Cards that work with multiple archetypes are extremely valuable for draft. A blue common Wizard with flying helps both a blue-red Wizard tribal archetype and a white-blue flying-matters archetype. These cross-archetype cards prevent the Limited environment from feeling siloed and repetitive.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #6: Iteration; MTGNexus: Draft Archetypes; press.exe: Interlocking Mechanical Space*

**DO use reprints and existing cards as design reference points.**
Study how existing cards are templated, costed, and designed. Before designing something, check if a version already exists — you will learn from what came before and avoid reinventing the wheel poorly.
*Source: Design 102*

### Playtesting Phase

**DO start playtesting as soon as commons are filled in.**
Do not wait for higher rarities. The all-common playtest will feel different from normal Magic (harder comebacks, more stalemates, more generic gameplay, higher repetition) — this is expected and normal. Do not judge an all-common playtest against a full-set environment.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #5: Initial Playtesting*

**DO ask five questions during every playtest.**
(1) What is fun? (2) What is not fun? (3) Are cards meeting design expectations? (4) Is anything confusing? (5) Are themes coming through? Take notes during or immediately after — memory fades quickly.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #5*

**DO treat playtesting as exploration, not competition.**
You are not there to win. You are there to experience the cards in your set. Play different colors, try different strategies, prioritize breadth of experience over depth of mastery. Require playtesters to experiment — assign different colors and insist on trying unfamiliar strategies.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #5; Nuts & Bolts #6*

**DO use the two-copy rule in initial playtesting.**
Limit card duplicates to two copies so you see more of the set in each test game. This gives you broader coverage and faster identification of problems.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #5*

**DO maintain Combo and Bombo lists.**
Track two-card interactions: the "Combo" list captures pairs with positive synergies, the "Bombo" list captures pairs with negative interactions and feel-bad moments. Cards with multiple Bombo interactions should be cut.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #6: Iteration*

**DO get outside perspectives.**
Designers become too close to their work. External feedback from people who have not been staring at the same file for months is invaluable. Ideal playtesters know the rules, give honest opinions, and can explain why they like or dislike something.
*Source: Design 102; Nuts & Bolts #9*

### Evaluation Phase

**DO use the seven evaluation questions as a structured self-assessment.**
(1) Can you describe the set in seven words or less? (2) Does every element serve the core vision? (3) Do the mechanics work together? (4) Is the set enjoyable to play? (5) Does it reward repeated exploration? (6) Does it introduce something genuinely new? (7) Does it still feel like Magic?
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #9: Evaluation*

**DO take emotional distance before evaluating.**
Step away from the design. Let time pass. Return with fresh eyes. You need objectivity that proximity to the work destroys. Be brutally honest about strengths and weaknesses.
*Source: Design 102; Nuts & Bolts #9*

**DO be willing to make significant changes even late in the process.**
If something is not working, cut it. The Gatecrash Orzhov mechanic was scrapped entirely and replaced with Extort. Attachment to individual designs over set cohesion is the mark of an amateur. "Kill your darlings" is not a suggestion — it is a requirement.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #5 (Gatecrash case study); Design 104*

## DON'Ts

### Card Design

**DON'T overcomplicate individual cards.**
The number one mistake of amateur designers. Too many abilities, too much text. A card should do one thing cleanly rather than three things awkwardly. If your card needs explanation beyond its rules text, it is too complicated. If you can remove an element without losing the core, remove it. "Perfection is achieved, not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away."
*Why it's a problem:* Complex cards are harder to evaluate, harder to develop, harder to play with, and often less fun despite seeming "cooler." They also consume complexity budget that could be spent on other cards.
*Source: Design 101 (Mistake #1); Design 104; Ten Principles of Good Design, Part 2*

**DON'T put abilities on a card that have no synergy with each other.**
Multiple abilities that do not reinforce each other are a red flag. Every ability on a card should work toward a unified purpose. A creature with flying and a graveyard-filling ability creates dissonance unless the set gives flying creatures a reason to care about the graveyard.
*Why it's a problem:* Players read the card and feel confused about what it is trying to do. The card does not create a clear strategic identity.
*Example:* A creature with "First Strike, When this creature deals combat damage, target player mills three cards" — the abilities have no mechanical connection.
*Source: Design 101 (Mistake #2)*

**DON'T design cards that do not work within the rules.**
Before you submit a card, verify that its mechanics actually function within the existing comprehensive rules. Interactions with the stack, timing, layers, state-based actions — these are not optional considerations. Consult rules experts when designing complex cards.
*Why it's a problem:* A card that requires a rules patch to function is a card that will confuse players and create judge headaches.
*Source: Design 101 (Mistake #4)*

**DON'T add "flavor add-ons" that do not impact gameplay.**
Extra abilities tacked on to make a card feel more flavorful but that rarely matter in actual play are dead weight. They add rules text, increase complexity, and give the illusion of depth without delivering it.
*Why it's a problem:* Every line of rules text has a cost — it makes the card harder to parse and consumes complexity budget.
*Source: Design 101*

**DON'T make a card that already exists.**
Before designing, search Scryfall or another database. If your card or something functionally identical has already been printed, you need to know. Redesigning an existing card wastes time and suggests insufficient knowledge of the game's history.
*Why it's a problem:* Duplicating existing designs means you are not adding to the game. It also means you may be unaware of why the original was designed the way it was.
*Source: Design 102*

### Mechanic Design

**DON'T design something just because "it hasn't been done."**
"It hasn't been done" is not a reason to do something. If the game has not done something in twenty years, odds are there is a reason. Novelty is not a virtue in itself. Innovation must serve the game, not just be different. Wait for the right problem where an unusual solution is elegant.
*Why it's a problem:* Chasing novelty produces mechanics that are interesting in theory but unfun in practice. Many "unexplored" design spaces are unexplored because they are bad.
*Source: Design 104 (The Explorer's Mistake)*

**DON'T try to be too literal with your mechanics.**
Do not try to capture every single detail of a concept. Focus on the essence, not exhaustive representation. A Vampire card does not need to represent every aspect of vampirism — it needs to capture the feeling of vampirism. Use fewer elements to convey the core idea.
*Why it's a problem:* Literal translations of concepts produce cards that are complex, wordy, and often unfun. The essence communicates better than the encyclopedia entry.
*Example:* A werewolf card that tries to mechanically represent the full moon, enhanced senses, pack behavior, silver vulnerability, and transformation all on one card.
*Source: Design 104 (The Artist's Mistake)*

**DON'T keep a great card that does not belong in this set.**
Evaluate cards in two stages: (1) Is this a good design? (2) Is this a good design for THIS specific set? A brilliant card that does not fit its set is not a good card for that set. Good cards will find a home eventually — do not force them. "No scene is worth a movie, no line is worth a scene."
*Why it's a problem:* Cards that do not serve the set's themes dilute the set's identity and consume slots that could go to cards that do serve it.
*Source: Design 104 (The Judge's Mistake); Nuts & Bolts #3*

**DON'T fight for everything during development.**
Identify your set's "bearing walls" — the core structural elements that must survive. Do not resist every proposed change. Be selective about which design elements you must absolutely protect. Development can improve the original design. Spend your political capital on what truly matters.
*Why it's a problem:* Designers who fight every change exhaust goodwill and lose the fights that actually matter. The most important elements get lost in the noise.
*Source: Design 104 (The Warrior's Mistake)*

**DON'T have too many named mechanics.**
The mechanic budget is 3-6 named/keyword mechanics per set, plus 1-4 unnamed themes. If there are only five or ten cards using a mechanic, cut it. If nothing references or cares about the mechanic, cut it. More mechanics means less support for each, which means none of them feel like they matter.
*Why it's a problem:* Each mechanic competes for card slots, complexity budget, and player attention. Spreading too thin means no mechanic gets enough support to be satisfying.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #15; MTG Salvation: Choosing Set Mechanics*

**DON'T create a mechanic that only works on one card type in one color.**
Good set mechanics need design space across multiple card types and multiple colors. A mechanic that only works on red creatures is not a set mechanic — it is a card. Evaluate: what card types can it go on? Which colors can use it? At what rarities can it appear?
*Why it's a problem:* Narrow mechanics cannot carry their weight in the set structure. They do not provide enough variety or strategic depth.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #17, Part 1*

**DON'T make the audience do something they do not want to do.**
If the optimal play pattern your mechanic creates is something players find tedious, frustrating, or unfun, the mechanic has failed regardless of how clever it is. The Odyssey set asked players to treat discarding cards as a resource, but players viscerally hated losing cards from their hand — the "fun" strategy was not fun.
*Why it's a problem:* Players will either avoid the mechanic (making it irrelevant) or use it and resent the experience. Neither outcome serves the set.
*Source: Design 103; Ten Principles of Good Design, Part 1 (Odyssey example)*

**DON'T design cards for the wrong audience.**
Know which psychographic (Timmy/Tammy, Johnny/Jenny, Spike, Vorthos, Mel) each card is for and design accordingly. A coin flip card designed to be tournament-competitive forces Spike to play a card Spike will hate. A build-around combo card with no setup complexity bores Johnny.
*Why it's a problem:* Players who are forced to interact with cards designed for a different audience feel resentful. The card fails to please the audience it reaches.
*Source: Design 103*

### Set Structure

**DON'T create factions or tribes with zero overlap.**
Ixalan's four tribes with no mechanical or strategic overlap made for a repetitive, shallow Limited environment. Drafters were locked into a tribe from pick one and never deviated. Each draft felt the same. Design cross-archetype cards and shared mechanical space so that drafters have interesting choices throughout the draft.
*Why it's a problem:* Without overlap, every draft plays out identically. Replayability — one of Magic's greatest strengths — is destroyed.
*Example:* Ixalan's Dinosaurs, Pirates, Merfolk, and Vampires had essentially no cards that bridged between tribes.
*Source: Lessons Learned, Part 4; MTGNexus: Draft Archetypes*

**DON'T design without a skeleton.**
Making cards ad hoc without a structural framework leads to missing mana curves, unbalanced color representation, insufficient removal, and absent draft signals. The skeleton ensures you have the right number of creatures, the right distribution of sizes, and the right spread of mechanics before you start filling in specifics.
*Why it's a problem:* You will end up with a collection of individually designed cards rather than a cohesive set. Entire categories of essential cards will be missing.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #2; MTG Salvation: Set Skeletons primer*

**DON'T let factions be unevenly sized.**
Ixalan had dinosaurs and pirates in three colors and merfolk and vampires in two. The imbalance created drafting problems where some factions had dramatically more support than others. If you use factions, keep them balanced in color access and card count.
*Why it's a problem:* Unbalanced factions mean some drafters have access to twice the card pool of others. The favored factions dominate.
*Source: Lessons Learned, Part 4*

**DON'T forget that every common must be playable.**
Under Play Booster structure with only 81 commons, there is no room for filler. Every common must contribute to the Limited environment. Push creature power levels at common and use modal effects and ETB abilities to increase card utility.
*Why it's a problem:* Dead commons in packs are feel-bad moments and thin out the playable card pool, making draft less interesting.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #16: Play Boosters*

### Color Pie

**DON'T violate the color pie.**
The color pie is the game's constitution — the structural principle that keeps mechanical identity coherent across tens of thousands of cards. Every color has defined strengths and weaknesses. A monowhite Counterspell or a monoblue creature removal spell is a violation that undermines the entire architecture.
*Why it's a problem:* Color pie violations remove the cost of choosing colors. If every color can do everything, color choice becomes meaningless, and the game's strategic foundation collapses. Mirrodin's all-generic-cost artifacts proved this — when every deck had access to every powerful card, the format became "The Blob," unfixable by removing any single card.
*Source: Design 101 (Mistake #3); Lessons Learned, Part 1 (Mirrodin); Play Design Lessons Learned*

**DON'T let a themed set collapse into one color.**
When designing a horror set, if everything "feels black," your set becomes mono-black. Innistrad's design rule was: "If something could be done in a color other than black, we would do it in that color." This kept the horror set from being mechanically monotone.
*Why it's a problem:* If one color absorbs the set's theme, that color becomes overdrafted, the other colors feel generic, and the set's mechanical identity is unevenly distributed.
*Source: C'mon Innistrad Part 1*

### Power Level

**DON'T undercost or overpush cards because they "feel cool."**
The most common power-level mistake among amateur designers is making cards too powerful because the fantasy is exciting. Playtest to calibrate card strength. Seek feedback from experienced developers. Power level is a design choice, not an accident.
*Why it's a problem:* Overpowered cards warp formats, reduce strategic diversity, and create negative play experiences. Underpowered cards are disappointing and waste design slots.
*Source: Design 101 (Mistake #5); Play Design Lessons Learned*

**DON'T design cheap, broad-impact permanents without extraordinary scrutiny.**
Three-mana planeswalkers (and analogous cheap, broad-impact designs) are inherently risky. Oko, Thief of Crowns invalidated essentially all relevant permanent types. Teferi, Time Raveler neutralized most instants. Cards that enter play early enough to warp games require extraordinary scrutiny, especially if they invalidate broad categories of opposing strategies.
*Why it's a problem:* Cheap permanents with broad answers leave no effective counterplay. The game warps around them because they come down before opponents can establish a position.
*Source: Play Design Lessons Learned (Oko case study)*

**DON'T deliberately power down sets to "open design space."**
The Battle for Zendikar through Core Set 2019 era deliberately reduced power levels. The lesson: low-power environments make the format more sensitive to slight power imbalances. A card like Smuggler's Copter becomes disproportionately dominant in a weak field. Power should be intentional and consistent, not artificially suppressed.
*Why it's a problem:* In a low-power environment, any card that is even slightly above the curve warps everything around it. The very problem you tried to avoid becomes worse.
*Source: Play Design Lessons Learned*

**DON'T design "safety valve" answers that are themselves problematic.**
Veil of Summer was designed as a safeguard against powerful blue and black cards. It became a format-warping problem itself. When designing answers, take varied, less aggressive approaches. An answer that is also a problem is not an answer.
*Why it's a problem:* You replace one problem with another. The cure becomes the disease.
*Source: Play Design Lessons Learned*

### Complexity

**DON'T violate New World Order at common.**
Three types of complexity — comprehension, board, and strategic — must all be kept low at common. Common cards should never be very complex. Red-flag every common that increases any type of complexity and ask whether that complexity is truly necessary. NWO was created to lower barriers for new players but ended up improving the game for everyone.
*Why it's a problem:* Complex commons overwhelm new players, create board states that even experienced players struggle to parse, and slow down gameplay.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #4: Higher Rarities; MTGNexus: So You Want to Build a Set*

**DON'T let complexity creep go unchecked.**
The natural tendency during design is for individual cards to accumulate more text as the process continues. Each addition seems small and justified, but the cumulative effect is a set that is exhausting to play. Monitor overall text density regularly and push back against it.
*Why it's a problem:* Complexity creep is invisible until it has gone too far. Each small addition is defensible in isolation, but the aggregate is overwhelming.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #3*

**DON'T put similar-but-different versions of a mechanic in the same set.**
The Lost Caverns of Ixalan had descend N, "if you've descended," and fathomless descent in the same set. Players felt the designers should have picked one. Variations that are close but not identical create confusion about which version a card uses and how they interact.
*Why it's a problem:* Players conflate the versions, leading to gameplay errors and frustration. The cognitive overhead of tracking three similar-but-distinct mechanics exceeds the benefit.
*Source: State of Design 2024; MTG Salvation forums*

### Limited Environment

**DON'T ignore Limited during design.**
Limited play ensures more cards are relevant, serves as a sampler of the set's mechanics and themes, and is where most players first experience the set. A set with a broken or boring Limited environment is a failed set regardless of how its Constructed cards perform.
*Why it's a problem:* Limited is the primary way players interact with new sets. If it is bad, the set's first impression is bad, and most of those cards will never be explored.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #12: Limited Mechanics*

**DON'T forget Bombs, Removal, and Evasion (BREAD).**
You need a fair amount of bombs (exciting powerful cards), removal (answers), and evasion (ways to break board stalls) spread evenly across all colors. Missing any of these in a color creates an unplayable Limited archetype.
*Why it's a problem:* Without removal, bombs dominate unchecked. Without evasion, board stalls become interminable. Without bombs, games lack excitement and drama.
*Source: MTGNexus: So You Want to Build a Set*

**DON'T make all your mechanics linear with no modularity.**
Understand where each mechanic falls on the modular-to-linear spectrum. Modular mechanics (flying, deathtouch) work independently in any deck. Linear mechanics (tribal synergies, specific card type requirements) require other specific cards. A set of all-linear mechanics creates tunnel-vision drafting where every pick is obvious. You need a mix.
*Why it's a problem:* All-linear environments remove draft skill and create repetitive gameplay. All-modular environments lack synergy and feel generic.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #12*

**DON'T be too cautious with mana fixing or too generous.**
Too little fixing and multicolor archetypes are unplayable. Too much fixing and players build five-color goodstuff piles that ignore archetype identity. Be especially cautious with lands that fix three or more colors.
*Why it's a problem:* Mana fixing is the thermostat of Limited. Set it too low and certain strategies are dead on arrival. Set it too high and all strategic differentiation melts away.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #15: Structural Support*

### Flavor and Creative

**DON'T follow literal accuracy over popular perception.**
Research deeply, but when popular perception of a concept diverges from literal accuracy, go with popular perception. "Don't let facts get in the way of a cool idea that feels right." Players do not care that real-world bats are not actually blind — they expect a bat creature to have some connection to darkness.
*Why it's a problem:* Pedantic accuracy creates cards that technically reflect the source material but do not match what players expect. Expectations are more powerful than facts.
*Source: A View from the Top*

**DON'T neglect the total aesthetic package.**
How a card reads, looks, and feels is not secondary to how it plays. Name, art, rules text, flavor text, templating — all contribute to the player experience. Aesthetics are not a decorative luxury but a fundamental component of how cards function. A mechanically identical card presented poorly will be received poorly.
*Why it's a problem:* Dissonance in aesthetics distracts and frustrates. Players respond to the total sensory package, not just the rules text in isolation.
*Source: Ten Principles of Good Design, Part 1*

**DON'T go too all-in on a narrow theme.**
Murders at Karlov Manor went too deep on its mystery/investigation theme, which was too narrow to support an entire card set. A theme needs enough breadth to produce varied gameplay across hundreds of cards. If the theme can only produce one kind of card or one kind of experience, it is too narrow.
*Why it's a problem:* A narrow theme exhausts its design space quickly, leading to repetitive cards and a monotonous play experience.
*Source: State of Design 2024*

## GOTCHAS

These are non-obvious traps that even experienced designers fall into. Things that seem right but are wrong.

### "Keywords add complexity" — WRONG.
Keywords SIMPLIFY. They reduce mental load through chunking. "Flying" is one cognitive unit instead of a full sentence. The shared vocabulary keywords create is a net positive for accessibility. Reminder text handles the learning curve. The fear of "adding another keyword" is usually misplaced — the question is whether the rules text without the keyword is harder to parse than learning the keyword.
*Source: Keyword Play; Keyword to the Wise*

### "If players keep doing something I didn't intend, players are wrong" — WRONG.
If players keep doing something you did not intend, the problem is your design, not the players. "You can't fight human nature and win." Change your game to match your players. Do not fight expectations — use them to your benefit.
*Source: Design 104; Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons*

### "A horror set should be mostly black" — WRONG.
Every themed set risks collapsing into one color. Innistrad's critical design rule was: if something could be done in a color other than black, do it in that color. Without this discipline, the set would have been mechanically lopsided and drafting would have been broken.
*Source: C'mon Innistrad Part 1*

### "My favorite card is too good to cut" — WRONG.
The set is more important than any individual card. Always. No exceptions. Good cards will find a home eventually. Forcing a card into a set because you love it, even when it does not serve the set's themes, is the single most common sign of an amateur designer. The Gatecrash Orzhov mechanic was completely scrapped and replaced — and the set was better for it.
*Source: Design 104; Nuts & Bolts #3; Nuts & Bolts #5*

### "Colorless artifacts are safe because they're not in any color" — WRONG.
Colorless artifacts with all-generic mana costs circumvent the color pie — one of the game's most important safety valves. When a broken card belongs to a specific color, it greatly limits how many decks can access it. Mirrodin's "The Blob" problem proved this catastrophically: every deck ran every busted artifact because nothing restricted access.
*Source: Lessons Learned, Part 1 (Mirrodin)*

### "More choices = more fun" — WRONG.
Choice is good to a point, then it becomes paralysis. Comprehension complexity, board complexity, and strategic complexity are all costs. When a player is thinking about the rules text rather than their strategic decisions, the design has failed. Good design is invisible — the infrastructure should get out of the way.
*Source: Ten Principles of Good Design, Part 1 (Principle #5: Unobtrusive)*

### "If everyone likes your game, you've succeeded" — WRONG.
"If everyone likes your game, but no one loves it, it will fail." Focus on creating elements that some players will love deeply, even if others dislike them. Broad appeal without passionate engagement produces a forgettable set.
*Source: The Big Picture*

### "Returning mechanics are lazy" — WRONG.
Good mechanics should return. The old philosophy of "use it and discard it" was wasteful. Cycling, Kicker, Flashback — these prove that revisiting great mechanics enriches the game. Old mechanics become new in new contexts. Flashback in Innistrad was recontextualized as "things returning from the grave" — mechanically identical, thematically fresh.
*Source: Keyword Play; C'mon Innistrad Part 1*

### "I need to be innovative everywhere" — WRONG.
Too much newness at once is disorienting. "Somewhat different is good. Too different is alienating." Every set needs a balance of the new and the familiar. Evergreen mechanics and known structures give players footing to explore new design space. Comfort anchors innovation.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #7; Design 104*

### "I can fix problems by adding more cards" — WRONG.
New material has to come at the cost of old material. You cannot solve a structural problem by adding more cards — the set has a fixed size. Every card added means another card removed. Solving problems means changing or cutting what exists, not expanding the file.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #6: Iteration*

### "Playtest power level can be evaluated in isolation" — WRONG.
During Oko's late redesign, the design team focused on individual ability changes without re-evaluating the card's total impact. Power level is holistic — changing one ability changes the card's relationship to the entire format. Always reassess a card's fundamental impact after any change, not just the changed element.
*Source: Play Design Lessons Learned*

### "A low-power environment is easier to balance" — WRONG.
Counter-intuitively, low-power environments are MORE sensitive to slight imbalances, not less. When the overall power level is low, any card that is even marginally above the curve warps the format because nothing else competes with it. Smuggler's Copter dominated specifically because nothing else in the environment could match it.
*Source: Play Design Lessons Learned*

## RED FLAGS

Warning signs during design and playtesting that indicate your set is off track. If you observe these, stop and diagnose the problem before continuing.

### Identity Red Flags

**You cannot describe the set in seven words or less.**
If the set's identity requires a paragraph to explain, it is not focused enough. A set without a clear, articulable purpose will drift and become incoherent.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #9*

**Your mechanics do not reinforce a unified theme.**
If your mechanics feel like they belong in different sets, they probably do. Every mechanic should serve the core vision. Mechanics that point in different directions create a set that feels like a random collection of cards.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #9 (Evaluation Question #2)*

**You struggle to explain why a player should be excited about this set.**
If you cannot articulate the emotional hook — the reason a player should care — the set lacks a compelling identity. "This set has new mechanics" is not a hook. "This set makes you feel like a mad scientist assembling creatures from spare parts" is a hook.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #9 (Evaluation Question #2)*

### Mechanic Red Flags

**A mechanic consistently gets "No" ratings after multiple playtests.**
If playtesters keep rejecting a mechanic, believe them. A mechanic that needs extensive explanation of why it is "actually fun" is not fun.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #6*

**Word count on cards keeps increasing.**
Complexity creep is happening. If cards keep getting more text with each iteration, you are adding complexity without adding fun. Push back. Simplify. Cut abilities.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #3; Nuts & Bolts #6*

**A mechanic only has 5-10 cards in the set.**
If you cannot find enough design space to put a mechanic on more than a handful of cards, it is not a set mechanic. Either expand it or cut it. A mechanic at that density will not register with players.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #15; MTG Salvation: Choosing Set Mechanics*

**A card has multiple Bombo interactions.**
If a card creates feel-bad anti-synergies with multiple other cards in the set, cut it. One Bombo is acceptable. Three or more is a pattern that indicates the card does not belong.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #6*

**Players keep using your mechanic in a way you did not intend.**
This is not a sign that players are wrong. This is a sign that your mechanic communicates differently than you think it does. Redesign the mechanic to match how players naturally want to use it, or embrace the unintended use if it is fun.
*Source: Design 104; Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons*

### Structural Red Flags

**One color is dramatically better than the others in playtesting.**
Color imbalance usually indicates either too much power concentration or insufficient removal in opposing colors. Check your skeleton — is the broken color getting better creatures, better removal, or better synergy pieces?
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #5; Nuts & Bolts #15*

**Every draft plays out the same way.**
If drafters are locked into the same archetypes every time and games follow predictable patterns, you lack cross-archetype cards and overlap between strategies. This was the core Ixalan problem.
*Source: Lessons Learned, Part 4*

**Board stalls happen frequently and go on too long.**
You need more evasion abilities at common (flying, menace, trample) and more ways to break through stalls. Check that each color has access to at least one form of evasion.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #15*

**One archetype is consistently underdrafted or overdrafted.**
The archetype either has too little support (underdrafted because it does not work) or too much (overdrafted because it is clearly the best). Check signpost uncommons, payoff density, and mana curve within that archetype.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #15; MTGNexus: Draft Archetypes*

**Your set doesn't feel like Magic.**
If playtesters describe the experience as "weird" or "unfamiliar" in a negative way, you may have pushed too far from the game's familiar foundations. Every set needs evergreen mechanics and known structures as anchoring points.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #9 (Evaluation Question #7)*

### Process Red Flags

**You are fighting every piece of feedback.**
If you find yourself defending everything and changing nothing, you have lost objectivity. Step back. Take a break. Remember: the Warrior's mistake is fighting for everything instead of fighting for the load-bearing walls.
*Source: Design 104 (The Warrior's Mistake)*

**You have not playtested in over a week.**
Design without playtesting is theory without evidence. Nothing will help you figure out what needs to get done better than playing with the cards. If you are designing in a vacuum, you are designing blind.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #5; Design 102*

**You are more excited about individual cards than the set as a whole.**
If your pitch for the set is "look at this cool card" rather than "look at this cool experience," you are thinking about trees instead of the forest. The set is more important than any individual card.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #3; Design 104*

**You keep adding mechanics to solve problems instead of cutting or changing existing ones.**
More mechanics means more complexity, more competition for card slots, and less support for each mechanic. If your current mechanics are not working, the answer is usually to fix or replace them, not to add more.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #18; Nuts & Bolts #6*

**You lost sight of the original design vision.**
As iteration proceeds, it is easy to make so many incremental changes that the set drifts away from its original purpose. Periodically review your original design goals. If the set no longer matches them, either course-correct or consciously redefine the vision.
*Source: Nuts & Bolts #6; Nuts & Bolts #9*

## Quick Reference: The Cardinal Rules

For when you need a one-page summary of what matters most.

1. **Simplicity above all.** Do the most with the least. If you can remove it without losing the core, remove it. *(Design 101; Design 104; Ten Principles)*

2. **Restrictions breed creativity.** Constraints are the engine of good design, not obstacles to it. *(Design 102; Every Nuts & Bolts article)*

3. **If your theme isn't at common, it isn't your theme.** Commons carry the set's identity. *(Design 102; N&B #3)*

4. **The set is more important than any individual card.** No exceptions. Kill your darlings. *(N&B #3; Design 104)*

5. **Fun beats everything.** If players are not enjoying themselves, nothing else matters. *(Ten Principles, Part 1)*

6. **Design for emotion.** Make players feel something. *(Stages of Design)*

7. **Don't fight human nature.** Design with the grain of psychology, not against it. *(Design 104; Twenty Years, Twenty Lessons)*

8. **Iterate with feedback.** Practice without feedback is repetition. *(N&B #5; N&B #6)*

9. **Playtesting is not playing.** You are there to experience the set, not to win. *(N&B #5)*

10. **Details matter disproportionately.** The difference between 2/3 and 3/2 can change everything. *(N&B #6; Ten Principles, Part 2)*
