## New Keyword Mechanics

### Mechanic 1: Forsake

**Rules Text Template:**
```
Forsake N (You lose N life and become forsaken. A forsaken player can't gain life.)
```

**Usage patterns:**
- As an additional cost: "As an additional cost to cast this spell, forsake 2."
- As a trigger: "When this creature dies, forsake 0."
- As an activated ability cost: "Forsake 1, {T}: Draw two cards."
- Forsake 0 = accept the forsaken status without life loss (the restriction alone is the cost)

**"Forsaken" is a player designation** (like the city's blessing from Ascend). Once you become forsaken, you are forsaken for the rest of the game. It cannot be removed. Cards can check "if you're forsaken" for additional effects and payoffs.

**Plain Language:** You pay life and permanently give up the ability to gain life. In exchange, you get effects that are above rate. Once you're forsaken, additional Forsake costs only charge you the life — the permanent restriction is already paid, so subsequent Forsake cards get effectively cheaper. "If you're forsaken" cards reward you for having committed.

**How It Serves the Set Identity:** The isekai theme is about giving up your old world to gain power in a new one. Forsake is the mechanical expression of "you can never go back." The players who came to Shandalark abandoned their old lives — willingly or not — and Forsake captures that irreversible commitment. It also creates a natural Spike/Johnny decision point: when is the right time to commit?

**Card Types It Appears On:** All types. Creatures (ETB or death triggers), instants/sorceries (additional cost or effect), enchantments (persistent Forsake-matters effects), artifacts (activated abilities).

**Target As-Fan:** <!-- TBD — needs skeleton work. Probably ~1.0-1.5 for Forsake cards, with additional "if you're forsaken" payoff cards raising the effective as-fan higher. -->

**Four-Test Evaluation:**
1. **Is it fun to play?** Yes — creates meaningful risk/reward decisions. The moment you choose to forsake is dramatic. Subsequent Forsake cards feel like doubling down. "If you're forsaken" payoffs create satisfying threshold moments.
2. **How many cards can support it?** **Wide.** The N parameter, the variety of usage patterns (cost, trigger, ability), and the "if you're forsaken" payoff cards create a large design space. Forsake can appear on anything from a common creature to a mythic bomb.
3. **Can it be tweaked and evolved?** Yes. Knobs: the N value (0-5+), what the card does in exchange, whether Forsake is a cost or a trigger, and the power level of "if you're forsaken" bonuses. Could also explore "forsake target opponent" or "each player forsakes" at rare+ if needed.
4. **What gameplay environment does it create?** Games where one or both players make a dramatic commitment partway through. Creates a natural "before forsaken / after forsaken" arc within each game. Aggro decks may forsake early and aggressively (life loss matters less when you're killing them). Control decks face a harder choice — they care more about life total and game length.

**Modularity vs. Linearity:** **Mostly modular.** Forsake cards work independently — you don't *need* "if you're forsaken" payoffs to make a Forsake card worth playing. But "if you're forsaken" cards create optional linear synergy for players who want to build around the status. Best of both worlds.

**Complexity Level:** Simple enough for common. "Lose N life and become forsaken" is one line. The forsaken designation is binary and permanent — no tracking required beyond "am I forsaken? yes/no." Comparable complexity to Ascend.

**Interaction With Other Set Mechanics:** <!-- TBD — depends on what other mechanics are in the set. -->

**Shard Alignment:** Forsake lives primarily in Black and Red, meaning it naturally spans Johnny (Grixis: UBR), Vorthos (Jund: BRG), and Spike (Esper: WUB) through their black components. "If you're forsaken" payoffs could appear in any color.

**Design Space Explored:**
- Common: "{1}{B} creature, 3/1. As an additional cost to cast this spell, forsake 1." (Above-rate body for the cost)
- Common: "{2}{R} Sorcery. Forsake 0. Deal 4 damage to target creature or planeswalker." (Premium removal, but you become forsaken)
- Uncommon: "{1}{B}{R} creature, 4/3. When ~ enters, forsake 2. If you're forsaken, ~ has menace and haste." (Payoff for being forsaken)
- Rare: "{3}{B}{B} Sorcery. Forsake 3. Destroy all creatures. If you're forsaken, instead destroy all creatures your opponents control." (Forsaken status upgrades the effect)
- Mythic: "{2}{B}{B} Legendary Creature, 5/5. Flying, lifelink. Forsake 0. If you're forsaken, ~ loses lifelink and gets 'Whenever ~ deals combat damage to a player, draw that many cards.'" (Lifelink is useless while forsaken — the card transforms its own ability)

## Returning Mechanics

### Under Consideration: Level Up

**Status:** Explored, generally approved, not committed. Revisit when mechanic slots are being finalized.

**Original Set:** Rise of the Eldrazi (2010)

**Storm Scale:** 8 — Rosewater: "Level up is the kind of mechanic I don't ever expect to see again, but I could imagine the perfect storm of elements that make it the ideal fit for an environment. Even then, we would have a major frame challenge ahead of us."

**Why It Fits This Set:** Isekai IS the perfect storm. "Level Up" is the core verb of the genre — SAO, Overlord, Solo Leveling, every gacha RPG. The keyword name alone communicates the set's identity through resonance and piggybacking (bestPractices.md Lessons 3-4). A creature that levels up on a plane where isekai'd players are gaining power requires zero explanation. The flavor does the mechanical communication work.

**Known Problems and Proposed Fixes:**

| Original Problem | Proposed Fix | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Confusing three-panel card frame | Two stages at common, three at uncommon+. Class enchantments proved two-stage leveling reads cleanly. | High — proven by Class |
| Starts at Level 0 (unintuitive) | Start at Level 1 | Trivial fix |
| Only goes on creatures (small design space) | Extend to enchantments (skills/spells being mastered) and artifacts (weapons/tools being refined) | Medium — needs testing |
| Colorless activation costs confused players | Use colored mana in Level Up costs. Already solved by spiritual successors (Evolved Sleeper, Ascendant Spirit). Adds a development knob. | High — solved problem |
| Small design space (three connected stages per card) | Two stages + more card types expands space. Two-stage is easier to design than three-stage. | Medium |
| Complexity at common (NWO concerns) | Two-stage common levelers with one threshold ("When this reaches Level 3, it gets +2/+2 and flying"). Full three-stage treatment at uncommon+. Follows NWO rarity gradient. | High — follows NWO |
| Counter type confusion (level counters vs. +1/+1 counters) | **Unresolved.** Level counters = new counter type competing with +1/+1 counters (NWO dislikes two counter types at common). Merging with +1/+1 counters means any +1/+1 counter source accidentally levels creatures — cool synergy or rules nightmare depending on execution. | Unresolved — key design decision |

**Interaction With Forsake:**
- **Potential synergy:** Levelers with a Forsake threshold at the top — "When this creature reaches Level 5, forsake 0." Incremental investment culminating in an irreversible transformation. Strong isekai beat: the character who trained for years then sacrificed something for their final form.
- **Potential tension:** Both mechanics tax the player — Level Up taxes mana each turn, Forsake taxes life. Together could feel like being squeezed rather than empowered. Needs playtesting.

**Open Question — Keyword vs. One-Off Designs:**
WotC's post-Level-Up approach has been individual leveler designs without keywording (Evolved Sleeper, Ascendant Spirit, etc.). Keywording "Level Up" is more resonant for this set — the keyword name IS a thematic statement — but it costs a mechanic slot and creates as-fan/NWO obligations. Alternative: use individual leveler-style cards without the keyword, freeing the mechanic slot but losing the resonance of the keyword name appearing on cards.

## Outstanding Design Questions

- [ ] Does every new mechanic pass the four-test evaluation?
- [ ] Can every named mechanic be executed simply enough for common?
- [ ] Do the mechanics create positive interactions (combos) more than negative ones (bombos)?
- [ ] Is the total mechanic count within budget (3-6 named + 1-4 unnamed)?
- [ ] Does the layering order make sense? Does the #1 priority mechanic truly define the set?
- [ ] Are mechanics concentrated in the right colors, or spread too thin?
- [ ] Is there at least one mechanic that serves each player psychographic (Timmy, Johnny, Spike)?
- [ ] Does any mechanic fight against another mechanic's gameplay?
- [ ] Are the as-fan targets achievable within the skeleton's card count constraints?
- [ ] Would any of these mechanics benefit from being an ability word instead of a keyword?
- [ ] Is there a returning mechanic that would gain new meaning in this set's context?
